# Separating real tyre leaks from data artefacts

The fleet-TPMS industry's central problem is that a pressure reading is a joint product of physics, hardware, process and analysis, and most "findings" confuse the four. For the Westline engagement the most useful prior is this: **raw pressure in a hot climate swings with temperature by far more than a healthy tyre loses to permeation**, so a Bengaluru-based analyst who skips temperature normalisation will mistake thermal cycling for leaks, and top-ups for self-healing. Gas-law behaviour is about 1 psi per 10 F (about 2%) of ambient change in passenger tyres, while natural loss is only about 3-5% a month, and truck tyres run 5-20 psi hotter when driven than cold. On top of that sit sensor faults (battery decay, cap-sensor spikes, wheel-position mismaps), telemetry artefacts (sleep gaps, duplicates, UTC vs IST day boundaries, split monthly files) and operational realities (wrong spec targets, hot-tyre inflation, unlogged swaps, manual top-ups). The evidence base is thin: most quantitative claims below come from trade press, vendors, patents or search-result summaries, not primary studies, and nothing was found on TyreSense itself. So the defensible approach for this brief is physics-first rules, cross-source checks (exports vs daily-km workbook vs transcript vs spec sheet), and explicit statements of what six vehicles over two months cannot show.

## Physics sets the baseline: thermal swings dwarf leaks

A sealed tyre follows the ideal gas law, so gauge pressure moves with absolute temperature. The consumer and trade rule is about **1 psi per 10 F, or roughly 2%**, and a worked 70 F to 20 F case gives 4.7 psi, matching the rule ([YouCanic](https://www.youcanic.com/tire-pressure-temperature-calculator)). The rule applies to ambient change, not to the extra heat from driving, which needs a separate offset ([Brake & Front End](https://www.brakeandfrontend.com/tpms-service-cold-tires/)). Linehaul truck tyres commonly run about **5-20 psi above cold pressure** when hot, and bleeding a hot tyre leaves it under-inflated once cool ([Truckinginfo](https://www.truckinginfo.com/articles/whats-this-cold-weather-doing-to-your-tire-pressure-updated)). Michelin advises checking at least three hours after stopping ([Michelin Professional](https://business.michelin.com.au/help-advice/managing-tyre-pressures)). ETRTO guidance, quoted by a UK retailer, allows hot pressure up to 20% over cold ([Bush Tyres](https://www.bushtyres.co.uk/?p=60605)). By contrast, natural permeation is small: a patent cites 3-5% a month ([Justia 11179977](https://patents.justia.com/patent/11179977)), a 1985 University of Michigan study found 0.5-1.0 psi a month at 70 F rising to about 1.5 at 90 F ([TRID](https://trid.trb.org/View/184503)), and Consumer Reports' year-long test found air losing 3.5 psi against 2.2 psi for nitrogen (paywalled; figures via search summary) ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/cars/tires/should-you-use-nitrogen-in-car-tires-a6260003694/)). Nitrogen therefore does not remove the need for checks. The rules of thumb themselves vary by source (1-2 psi per 10 F, 0.45-0.5 psi per 1,000 ft of altitude), so any normalisation needs a sensitivity check.

The analyst inference, not a sourced standard, is to convert gauge to absolute pressure (adding about 13.1-13.2 psi at Bengaluru's roughly 920 m, my calculation), scale by absolute temperature to a common reference, and use the sensor's own temperature channel rather than ambient whenever the export has one. Ambient is only adequate for parked, thermally settled samples. The notes also suggest that residual error after correction of about 0.5-1 psi is plausible, which is comparable to a month of permeation, so slow-leak claims below roughly 1 psi per week need strong uncertainty language. Altitude is minor for this fleet but non-zero: a tyre filled in Bengaluru and driven to the coast would read about 1.5 psi higher (arithmetic from the cited 0.5 psi per 1,000 ft).

Failure modes then differ mainly by rate and by whether the loss survives temperature correction. A European Rubber Journal piece notes a nail can hold a tyre for an hour, a day or a week and then let go in minutes ([ERJ](https://www.european-rubber-journal.com/article/2081707/deflation-and-punctures-a-tpms-debate)); a Bridgestone patent separates natural temperature drop from puncture by tracking the slope of temperature-compensated pressure ([US9162541](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9162541)). Published fleet-leak patents split rapid loss (more than 20% in 24 hours) from slow loss (days to weeks, regression slope) ([US 12583268](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12583268)), but these are unvalidated claims, and no source gives psi-per-hour thresholds for valve, rim or bead leaks. The signatures below are therefore my construction from the physics and need tuning on Westline's own data.

| Mechanism | Expected signature in compensated data (analyst inference) |
|---|---|
| Chronic under-inflation | Persistently below cold spec, flat or slowly falling, offset since last service |
| Over-inflation | Persistently above spec after correction, often from filling hot |
| Slow leak | Negative slope between top-ups, order of psi per day to week, sawtooth with falling peaks |
| Valve, rim or bead leak | Recurring top-ups on one wheel position; intermediate rate |
| Puncture or blowout | Step or ramp loss over minutes to hours, no recovery on cooling, possible dropout |
| Overload or dragging brake | Heat rising with pressure reading normal, so temperature adds information pressure misses |

## Consequences are real but the numbers are soft

Under-inflation matters, but quoted costs depend on basis and source. TMC/ATA figures via FleetOwner say constant 20% under-inflation cuts tyre life about 30% and 40% under-inflation about 50% ([FleetOwner](https://www.fleetowner.com/equipment/brakes-tires-wheels/article/21164410/tires-in-real-time-managing-psi-and-tread-depth)), while a Michelin test reports roughly 20% faster wear ([Driving for Better Business](https://www.drivingforbetterbusiness.com/articles/michelin-warns-fleets-on-cost-of-driving-on-under-inflated-tyres/)). Fuel penalties are smaller: EPA SmartWay puts 10 psi under-inflation at 0.5-1% of truck fuel economy (via search summary) ([EPA](https://19january2017snapshot.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2016-06/documents/420f16035.pdf)), and ATMA uses about 0.2% per psi for petrol cars ([Daily Pioneer](https://dailypioneer.com/news/low-tyre-pressure-costs-india-rs-4500-cr-in-fuel-every-year-atma)). A 2012 NHTSA study, read only through secondary summaries, found tyres 25% or more under-inflated about three times as likely to be cited as critical pre-crash events ([ERJ](https://www.european-rubber-journal.com/2012/05/15/nhtsa-report-tyre-underinflation-low-tread-contribute-to-highway-accidents)). A FleetOwner column attributing to FMCSA that only about 15% of roadside tyre events involve sudden damage, the rest being missed slow leaks, is unverified against the report ([FleetOwner](https://www.fleetowner.com/industry-perspectives/ideaxchange/article/21133003/lets-talk-tires)). Indian cost-share claims are mutually inconsistent: tyres at about 7% ([FleetX](https://www.fleetx.io/tyre-management)), about 10% ([Pulse/ETAuto](https://www.pulse.bot/transportation/news/indias-energy-vulnerability-has-a-tyre-problem-f0c969bf-ad3c-49e1-a6e6-a3fd78253e64/)) and 20-30% ([FleetX blog](https://blog.fleetx.io/how-to-manage-tyre-operations-inventory-effectively)), all vendor or trade sources. A planning range of 10-30% depending on definition is defensible, but a single figure is not. Fuel-only business cases overstate; tyre life and downtime are the larger levers.

Under-inflation is endemic but "wrong pressure" is not only low pressure. ATMA/ITTAC's survey of more than 130,000 passenger-vehicle tyres, reported through news coverage rather than the study, found 53% at least 10% low ([Tyre Trends](https://tyre-trends.com/auto-trends/low-tyre-pressure-costs-india-inr-45-bln-in-wasted-petrol-each-year-says-atma-survey)), and an older Apollo car survey found 22% at recommended pressure, about 44% over-inflated and 34% under-inflated ([Tyrepress](https://tyrepress.com/2010/01/apollo-registers-tyre-pressure-safety-concerns-on-indian-market)). Neither is a truck-fleet compliance rate. Load and mismatch effects are better established: Michelin guidance via FleetOwner says dual pressure mismatches above 5 psi cause circumference differences and irregular wear, and a 5 psi gap on steer tyres causes pull, while a 20% low truck tyre should be treated as flat. TMC-attributed figures say 22% of inspected duals are mismatched by more than 5 psi, but the primary report was not confirmed ([Automotive Fleet](https://www.automotive-fleet.com/340145/match-dual-tires-for-optimal-miles)). Overloading, which behaves like under-inflation (heat rises while pressure looks normal), is widely asserted in India; IFTRT's estimate that 70% of national-permit trucks overload comes via a magazine ([Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/trend/death-on-wheels-2197)). Tyre-burst accident counts found were from 2014 (9,748 accidents, 3,371 deaths) ([Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/amp/story/india%2Faccidents-due-tyre-bursts-serious-2152334)); no newer MoRTH data were located. Nothing quantitative was found on potholes, monsoon effects, rotation or alignment, so those should be treated as qualitative.

## Sensor and telemetry faults masquerade as tyre events

Hardware and pipeline artefacts are the main way a clean-looking dataset misleads, though the sourcing is the weakest in this report: retailer pages, forums and vendor blogs, with no TyreSense documentation. Sensor batteries are sealed and non-replaceable, last about 5-10 years by one retailer ([Denso](https://www.densoautoparts.com/what-causes-tpms-sensors-to-fail)), and as voltage drops the signal weakens and becomes intermittent, which can produce false low-pressure alerts ([Brake & Front End](https://www.brakeandfrontend.com/identifying-tpms-battery-issues/)). Cap (external) sensors read temperature about 20-30 F cooler than internal ones with similar pressure in one informal 12-sensor RV test ([RVTravel](https://www.rvtravel.com/final-results-of-tpms-comparison-tests/)), and owners report spurious cap-sensor temperature spikes of 300 F or more ([AirForums](https://www.airforums.com/threads/tst-tpms-false-warnings.1434843/post-16141011)); both are anecdotal. The practical implication, by inference, is to judge a sensor against its own history and peers, not an absolute threshold. No authoritative source covers drift rates, stuck-value signatures, unit mix-ups or sentinel codes (0, -1, 255, 65535), so these must be tested empirically: zero or sub-ambient pressure on a moving vehicle is physically impossible, and a column clustering near 7-9 versus 100-130 versus 700-900 flags bar versus psi versus kPa (my arithmetic).

Position mapping is a second trap. Direct sensors carry unique IDs that the receiver maps to wheel positions, and without a relearn after rotation or replacement pressures appear under the wrong wheel ([Autel](https://autel.us/what-does-it-mean-to-relearn-a-tpms-sensor-when-does-it-need-to-be-done-and-how-do-i-do-it/)); fleet telematics also suffers duplicate or orphaned asset identities ([Autosist](https://autosist.com/blog/why-fleet-tracking-data-shows-wrong-vehicle/), vendor). By inference, a swap shows as a date after which a position adopts another axle's level and temperature behaviour, and a sensor ID appearing on two vehicles on overlapping dates is a red flag. No mis-mapping rates exist.

Gaps and timestamps are the third. Sleep behaviour is manufacturer-specific (a Porsche NHTSA bulletin has sensors sleeping five minutes after standstill ([NHTSA TSB](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2015/MC-10198524-9999.pdf))), and quoted cadences of 30-120 seconds driving and 5-10 minutes to hours parked come from patents and explainers, not datasheets ([TPMS RF theory](https://www.underhoodservice.com/tpms-radio-frequency-theory-and-operation/)). So overnight silence is not a fault, but gaps during known driving are, and interpolating across sleep can invent leak slopes (inference). Raw streams carry duplicates and out-of-order packets, and developers advise normalising to UTC before deduplicating ([routematching](https://routematching-com.pages.dev/gps-data-preprocessing-cleaning-fundamentals/outlier-removal-in-raw-telematics-streams/)). My own inference, directly relevant here, is that a UTC export for an IST (UTC+5:30) fleet will push evening events onto the next date and split trips across the monthly per-vehicle files; the Data Dictionary should settle this. Odometer reconciliation is standard in fleet platforms (ECM vs GPS mismatch, spikes, stagnation, negative deltas), but the source page was unreadable and no typical GPS-vs-CAN gap was found ([Perspio](https://help.perspio.io/help/meter-violation-report)). Finally, FMVSS 138 requires a warning at 25% below placard ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2005/04/08/05-6741/federal-motor-vehicle-safety-standards-tire-pressure-monitoring-systems-controls-and-displays)), a coarse benchmark for light vehicles; whether it covers heavy trucks is from background knowledge only and unverified, and per-position pressures imply a direct system because indirect systems cannot report them.

## Operations and process decide whether the data means anything

Even perfect data reflects management practice. Manual checking is error-prone: hot readings overstate cold pressure by up to about 15% ([Fleet Equipment](https://www.fleetequipmentmag.com/?p=141461)), stick gauges vary unless calibrated against a master, and car gauges can be unreliable on commercial tyres above 100 psi ([Gulf Oil](https://master.gulfoilltd.com/blog/maintenance-advice/how-to-check-truck-tyre-pressure)). US EPA material, summarised by search, says only 8% of drivers gauge before each trip and under half of combination-truck tyres are within 5% of target ([EPA](https://19january2017snapshot.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2016-06/documents/420f16035.pdf)); I found no Indian truck equivalent. Topping up clears an alert for a day without fixing a leak ([Oxmaint](https://oxmaint.com/industries/fleet-management/tpms-alert-to-fleet-work-order-automated-tire-response), vendor). Required cold pressure depends on size, load range, axle position and load, so a single fleet target or the wrong spec-sheet row makes correct tyres look faulty ([Tire Review](https://www.tirereview.com/understanding-and-coaching-fleets-about-tires-and-inflation-pressure/)). Indian truck economics add a record-keeping problem: more than 70% of truck tyres are retreaded at least once ([Tyre Trends/ICRA](https://www.tyre-trends.com/spotlight/70-percent-truck-tyres-in-india-are-retreaded-once-icra)), and manual registers of tyre ID, position and rotation history are error-prone ([FleetX](https://blog.fleetx.io/top-5-benefits-of-utilizing-the-fleetx-tyre-management-module/)). Unlogged swaps, mixed sizes or sensors left on the wrong vehicle corrupt later analysis (inference).

On TPMS value, evidence is positive but conditional. A US DOT field test linked TPMS and auto-inflation to a fuel gain of about 1.4-1.8% and under-one-year ROI for at least one fleet, though secondary reports call parts of it inconclusive ([HDT](https://www.truckinginfo.com/news/dot-study-proves-roi-on-tire-inflation-and-pressure-monitoring-systems)). Vendor payback claims of 4-6 months are modelled assumptions ([Oxmaint](https://oxmaint.com/industries/fleet-management/tire-pressure-monitoring-tpms-fuel-efficiency-guide-2026)), alert-fatigue figures are unsourced vendor assertion ([Motive](https://gomotive.com/blog/cost-of-delayed-action-fleet-management/)), and the real determinant is whether alerts become work orders. India's AIS-151 mandate for heavier vehicles is claimed by market-research firms without official confirmation ([Mordor Intelligence](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/india-aftermarket-tpms-market)). No Indian TPMS case study with measured outcomes, and no split of alerts into sensor faults versus leaks, was found.

## Analytical pitfalls that turn six vehicles into false certainty

The notes yield a consistent set of traps, and most are generic statistics applied to this setting, not TPMS-specific findings. Simpson's paradox is documented in general ([PMC7175433](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7175433)); here it implies pooling vehicles with different set-points and duty cycles can reverse the sign of a pressure-vs-temperature or pressure-vs-km trend, so stratify by vehicle and position and compare within vehicle. Survivorship bias follows by inference: replaced tyres, burst tyres and failed sensors vanish from later months, and a repaired leak looks healthy afterwards, so report the denominator per month. Averages mix hot and cold states and hide bimodality, so prefer medians and percent-time below threshold on cold-normalised data. Inner and outer duals should never be averaged; a persistent gap may be a valve extension or sensor issue. Outliers can be real (blowouts, braking heat, re-inflation overshoot), so remove only physically impossible values, log every exclusion and show results with and without. "Underinflated" itself is defined inconsistently, from 8 psi below recommendation to 25% below, so prevalence figures from different studies are not comparable ([Tire Review](https://www.tirereview.com/defining-truck-tire-underinflation/)).

Normalisation by distance needs care. Leaks are mostly time-driven and punctures and heat effects distance-driven, so present psi/day and per-1,000 km views separately, flag vehicle-periods with near-zero km, and confirm the daily-km workbook covers the same plates (formatting such as KA22AD7730 vs spaced forms), dates and time zones as every export. Deduplicate on vehicle, position and timestamp, check month-boundary overlaps, and treat a vehicle with fewer observed days as lower-weight, not healthier. Fit slopes separately between re-inflation events, since a regression across a step gives a wrong slope, and give 95% intervals with sensitivity to reference temperature and the 1-vs-2 psi per 10 F coefficient.

The notes' client-narrative guidance is inference, not literature, but it fits an assessment that rewards cross-checking. "Sensors are faulty" can be tested: faults confined to certain wheels or vehicles, stepwise recovery after top-ups, correlation with time of day, flat-lining or missingness, or a mis-set target each point to a different cause. "We check daily" can be compared against recovery steps; "drivers ignore alerts" against pressure recovery after alerts, separating no-response from no air access; "the spec is right" against fitted size, load index and axle load; and a whole vehicle sitting steadily below target with no recovery suggests a wrong target before a leak. Transcript and spec statements are claims to test, not measurements. Per the notes, the data cannot reveal leak root cause without inspection, who re-inflated, load and route conditions unless supplied, tread or age, financial impact without cost data and a causal design, fleet-wide prevalence from six vehicles, events during sensor sleep or missing exports, or whether a tyre was truly cold at each reading.

## Conclusion

The sharpest takeaway is a ranking of suspects: before attributing a pattern to a leaking tyre, rule out in order a wrong spec target, a thermal or hot-versus-cold artefact, a top-up masking effect, a mapping or timestamp error and a sensor fault, because each of those is more common in the evidence than a true fast failure and each leaves a distinct, testable signature. Tyres losing 1 psi a month are normal; the interesting findings will be steps, recurring top-ups at one position, and heat without pressure loss.

The evidence also bounds what a report should claim. Nearly all quoted prevalence, cost and ROI figures here are secondary, vendor-sourced or from other countries and vehicle classes, and none should be presented as Westline's benchmark. Reports should label each statement as measured, derived, reported or hypothesis, quote ranges, and list the data that would close gaps: the tyre temperature channel, an ambient source, service and inflation logs, load and GVW, and fitted tyre specs by position.
